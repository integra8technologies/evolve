Product strategy

Product discovery starts with listening.

Understand the people and the work before deciding what to build.

Product strategy3 minute read

Begin with people's experience

Product conversations often begin with a proposed feature or a preferred technology. Those ideas may be useful, but they are not yet evidence that the underlying problem is understood. Discovery gives a team the chance to learn how people work today, where they encounter friction, and what they already do to get around it.

Listen for the details behind a request. Ask what someone was trying to accomplish, what made it difficult, and how they know the task is complete. Observing a real workflow can reveal steps and constraints that are easy to miss in a short description.

Look for patterns, not just opinions

One person's experience is valuable, but a product decision should consider more than the loudest request. Talk with people who represent different roles and circumstances. Compare what people say with what happens in practice, and note where the same obstacle appears repeatedly.

Then make your assumptions explicit. Which users face the problem? How often does it occur? What would improve if the problem were easier to solve? These questions help the team separate an important need from an attractive but untested solution.

Turn what you learn into a next step

Discovery is not a reason to delay delivery indefinitely. Its purpose is to help the team choose a useful next step with less guesswork. That may be a small experiment, a prototype to discuss with users, or a focused product release that tests the riskiest assumption.

Share what the team heard and what remains uncertain. When discovery stays connected to real decisions, it helps teams build less on assumption and more on what people actually need.

Key takeaway

Listen to people, observe the work, and use repeated patterns to decide what is worth solving first.
