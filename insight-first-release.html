Product strategy

Make the first release a learning tool.

A first release is most valuable when it helps your team answer an important question about the product.

Product strategy3 minute read

Start with the decision, not the feature list

A long list of features can make a plan look thorough, but it does not guarantee that the finished product will resolve the uncertainty behind it. Before deciding what to build, identify the decision the release should help you make. Are people able to complete the core task? Does the new workflow fit the way a team actually works? Is the problem important enough that customers will change their habits?

These questions give the team a shared outcome to work toward. They also help separate assumptions that need evidence now from ideas that can wait until there is more to learn.

Make the smallest complete experience

A useful first version is not necessarily the version with the fewest screens. It is the smallest complete experience that lets a real user attempt the important task and gives the team meaningful feedback. Keep the essential journey intact, including the points where people need clarity, confidence, or a way to recover from a mistake.

Then make deliberate choices about what can come later. A secondary report, an advanced setting, or an additional integration may be valuable eventually without being necessary to test the product's central promise.

Agree on what you will learn

Before release, decide what evidence will be useful and how you will collect it. Combine observation and feedback from users with signals that show whether the task was completed. Define who will review the findings and when the team will make its next decision.

When the results are mixed, resist the urge to immediately add more features. Look for where people hesitate, what they expected to happen, and which assumptions the evidence supports or challenges. The goal is not to prove that the original idea was right. It is to make the next product decision with more confidence.

Key takeaway

Choose one important assumption, build a complete path to test it, and decide in advance what you will do with what you learn.
